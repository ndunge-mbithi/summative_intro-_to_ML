\small\textbf{\textit{Abstract}---}Motivated by the large share of undiagnosed diabetes in rural sub-Saharan Africa, where questionnaire-based triage is far more scalable than laboratory testing, I compare traditional machine learning and deep learning for classifying self-reported diabetes status (no diabetes, prediabetes, diabetes) from 25 population health indicators in the raw 2015 CDC Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System. The data comprise 441,456 records, verified against the official codebook, of which 440,658 have a valid label. Using a leakage-safe pipeline that keeps respondents with missing predictors, I ran 17 tracked experiments covering logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting, and TensorFlow/Keras networks built with the Sequential and Functional APIs and fed through \texttt{tf.data}. The experiments varied hyper-parameters, engineered features, class weighting and regularisation. On a held-out test set of 66,099 respondents, tuned gradient boosting achieved a macro ROC-AUC of 0.800 and a macro average precision of 0.479. A wide-and-deep network matched it (0.800 and 0.478), and logistic regression followed closely (0.794 and 0.469). Diabetes was moderately well discriminated (ROC-AUC 0.843), but prediabetes was not (average precision 0.036). Decision rules mattered more than algorithms: a validation-selected $F_2$ threshold raised diabetes sensitivity from 0.17 to 0.85, at the cost of flagging 39\% of respondents. Error, explainability and subgroup analyses point to label limitations, reverse causation and uneven sensitivity across age, income and racial groups. The models are research tools, not diagnostic systems.

\smallskip
\textbf{\textit{Index Terms}---}diabetes, BRFSS, class imbalance, gradient boosting, deep learning, population health, screening.
