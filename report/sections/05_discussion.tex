\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the models ended up so close}
My hypothesis held in direction, but the effect turned out smaller than I expected. Tuned gradient boosting gave the best threshold-free performance, a well-regularised network matched it on ROC-AUC, and logistic regression was only about 0.01 behind in average precision. This agrees with the clinical review of Christodoulou \emph{et al.}~\cite{christodoulou2019} and with the tabular benchmarks~\cite{grinsztajn2022,shwartzziv2022}, and my experiments suggest a reason. In these data, most of the signal is additive. The best network was the one containing a logistic regression, engineered interactions helped neither paradigm, and every network landed between 0.473 and 0.480 macro AP whatever its depth or learning rate.

The tuning results show where useful complexity runs out. Giving the models more capacity, whether deeper forests or larger boosted trees, raised their scores on training folds by as much as 0.18 without improving held-out performance, and in both searches the winning configuration was the most heavily regularised one on offer. The boosting learning curve tells the same story. Training and validation loss settled with a small, steady gap (0.357 against 0.370) and the validation curve never turned upwards, so the model sits close to the point where extra variance stops buying lower bias. My diabetes AUC of 0.843 is also in line with the 0.82--0.83 that Chowdhury \emph{et al.}~\cite{chowdhury2026} found on the binary derivative, close to their estimated ceiling. Taken together, this suggests that the limit lies in what the survey measures rather than in the algorithms, and it adds to my doubts about reported AUCs near 0.99 on the same variables~\cite{majcherek2025}.

\subsection{Thresholds matter more than algorithms}
The single most consequential decision in this project was the decision rule, not the choice of model. With default decisions, every model was more than 85\% accurate while finding at most a fifth of diabetes cases and no prediabetes cases at all, a textbook case of accuracy misleading under imbalance~\cite{saito2015}. Both class weighting and tuned decision multipliers improved minority-class recall, but weighting did so at the cost of average precision, whereas adjusting the multipliers after training left the probability estimates untouched. I prefer the second approach because it keeps ranking quality separate from where the line is drawn, and the $F_2$ threshold shows what drawing that line entails. It found 85\% of diabetes cases, but only by flagging almost two in five respondents. Weighting recall more heavily makes sense when a missed case means an untreated disease and a false alarm means a confirmatory blood test. Even so, I would not recommend any clinical threshold on the strength of these results alone.

\subsection{What the errors reveal about the labels}
The error analysis changed how I see the false positives. Many wrongly flagged people look, on paper, like people with diabetes, and because the label records whether someone was \emph{told}, undiagnosed cases count as negatives; some of these ``errors'' may be correct. Prediabetes suffers most, since it only appears in the data if someone happened to be tested and informed. That probably explains why it occupied a blurred middle ground between its neighbours, and why its average precision of 0.036 says as much about the label as about the models. The data also blur cause and effect. Poorer self-rated health, a recent check-up, a cholesterol test and drinking less are all things that become more likely \emph{after} a diagnosis, and Xie \emph{et al.}~\cite{xie2019} saw the same check-up pattern. The feature importances therefore describe what a classifier can exploit in cross-sectional data, not what causes diabetes~\cite{strobl2007}.

\subsection{Representativeness and fairness}
The BRFSS is large and nationally weighted, but its self-reported prevalence differs from surveys with physical measurements~\cite{pierannunzi2013}. Survey-weighted test metrics stayed close to the unweighted ones (weighted macro ROC-AUC 0.828), so my conclusions do not appear to be an artefact of the sample's age structure. The subgroup results are more troubling: one threshold gave recall of 0.21 for adults under 45 against 0.63 in the lowest income band, and the groups with the most diabetes were the hardest to rank. Because getting a diagnosis depends on access to care, the quality of the labels may itself differ between groups~\cite{obermeyer2019}. These disparities are descriptive and do not prove that the model is unfair, but they would need to be addressed before any real use.

\subsection{What this means for rural sub-Saharan Africa}
Three findings bear directly on my mission. First, a simple model is enough: logistic regression came within about 0.01 of the best, runs on any phone, and has coefficients a community health worker can understand. Second, choosing a threshold is a decision about resources. Catching 85\% of cases meant flagging 39\% of people, which is only sensible where clinics can supply confirmatory tests on that scale. Third, and most important, ``ever told'' labels undercount the people with least access to care, which describes the rural communities where most diabetes goes undiagnosed~\cite{idfafrica2021,mohamed2018}; a model trained on such labels would learn to miss the very people it is meant to find. Any African deployment would have to begin by collecting laboratory-confirmed labels locally and revalidating the model on them.

\subsection{Limitations and next steps}
The searches also used a 100,000-row subsample, the data cover only US adults in 2015, and I did not assess calibration. The most valuable next steps are to validate against laboratory-defined diabetes, for instance HbA1c measurements in NHANES, so that undiagnosed cases are labelled correctly; to use design-based estimation with the survey strata; to add calibration checks and group-specific thresholds; and to test whether the models hold up on later survey years.
