\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

I set out to learn how well traditional machine learning and deep learning can classify diabetes status from population health indicators, and how the two approaches differ. Working with 440,658 respondents to the raw 2015 BRFSS, keeping prediabetes as its own class and imputing missing answers rather than discarding them, I found that both approaches reach a similar and fairly modest ceiling. On the held-out test set, tuned gradient boosting achieved a macro ROC-AUC of 0.800 and a macro average precision of 0.479, a wide-and-deep Keras network matched its ROC-AUC, and logistic regression came within about 0.01 while training in seconds and remaining fully interpretable. Diabetes itself was identified reasonably well (ROC-AUC 0.843), but self-reported prediabetes was almost indistinguishable from its neighbouring classes.

The comparison is fully reproducible, from a codebook-verified data file to identical results on repeated runs. The decision rule, far more than the algorithm, determined whether a model found one diabetes case in six or five in six, and the error and subgroup analyses exposed label gaps, reverse causation and unequal sensitivity that headline accuracy would have hidden. The main limitations are the self-reported labels, the cross-sectional design, evaluation that does not fully account for the survey design, and the use of a single year of data.

For my own path, the most important lesson is that questionnaire-based screening can help, but only if the labels it learns from are trustworthy. The next step towards my mission is to validate models like these against laboratory-confirmed glycaemic status, to calibrate them and set thresholds group by group, and to test them on locally collected data from sub-Saharan African communities before considering any use in rural screening.
